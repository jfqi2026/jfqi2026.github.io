<div id="talk-mio-murao" class="talk-entry talk-detail" markdown="1">

#### Quantum Learning with Higher-Order Quantum Computation
{: .talk-title }

Mio Murao · The University of Tokyo
{: .talk-byline }

Efficiently learning the properties of quantum objects is one of the most promising applications of quantum computers. In this talk, I will introduce higher-order quantum algorithms for learning unknown quantum channels. These algorithms are based on higher-order quantum computation, in which a black-box quantum channel is transformed into another quantum channel or a numerical quantity characterizing its properties. In this framework, a quantum computer acquires “quantum data” through black-box queries and processes it coherently, without reconstructing a full classical description of the channel. Quantum learning in this framework aims not only to extract classical information about unknown quantum channels but also to transform them into other quantum channels that can be used for subsequent quantum information processing. After an overview of higher-order quantum learning algorithms developed by our group, I will discuss some of our recent results on quantum algorithms for learning unknown quantum channels in more detail.

</div>

<div id="talk-nicolas-treps" class="talk-entry talk-detail" markdown="1">

#### Generating and certifying non-Gaussian states in time-frequency modes of light
{: .talk-title }

Nicolas Treps · LKB, CNRS, Sorbonne University
{: .talk-byline }

The time-frequency modes of light form a collection of quantum harmonic oscillators that can carry and process quantum information. Gaussian states can exhibit entanglement across many such modes, yet protocols based entirely on Gaussian states, operations and measurements can be simulated efficiently on a classical computer. This motivates the search for ways to prepare non-Gaussian states and certify their quantum properties. We will discuss how subtracting a single photon from a selected mode can produce a non-Gaussian state, and how coupling light to a two-level system offers a complementary route. We will then show how to certify specific properties without reconstructing the full state. In particular, measurements in a single mode basis can reveal entanglement that persists under any choice of mode basis.

</div>

<div id="talk-perola-milman" class="talk-entry talk-detail" markdown="1">

#### The quantum computational description of the electromagnetic field
{: .talk-title }

Pérola Milman · MPQ, CNRS, University Paris Cité
{: .talk-byline }

Quantum information can be encoded in states of the quantum electromagnetic field, with computational operations implemented through physical processes. Within this correspondence, computational resources—such as computational basis states and Clifford and non-Clifford gates—are constructed from physical resources. In quantum optics and bosonic quantum information more generally, these resources are intimately connected to the notion of nonclassicality of physical states. However, establishing an exact mapping between the computational and physical spaces faces fundamental challenges. From a physical perspective, the quantum field is composed of bosons, which are indistinguishable particles whose states are symmetric under exchange, whereas qubits or qudits are distinguishable systems that can be individually addressed. Moreover, from a computational perspective, qubits and qudits are defined in finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, while the electromagnetic field admits unbounded observables with continuous spectra.

In this talk, I will show that a formal correspondence between the descriptions of quantum computers and the electromagnetic field can be established by explicitly incorporating a quantum phase reference into the quantum-optical formalism. Within this description, bosonic states obey a photon-number superselection rule, which forbids coherences between different total photon-number sectors. In recent works, we have shown that this approach enables us to identify computational resources encoded in quantum states of the field [1], determine the origin of quantum metrological advantage [2], and unveil the physical origin of non-Gaussianity, a property closely associated with nonclassicality and potential quantum computational advantage [3].

I will detail the necessary and sufficient conditions for the emergence of the conventional continuous-variable description of the quantum field [3] and use these results to show that it is always possible to map, with arbitrary precision, a quantum computer onto the electromagnetic field and vice versa. Furthermore, I will demonstrate that, for any encoding of quantum information in the bosonic Hilbert space, resources that are provably efficiently simulatable on a classical computer can always be represented by positive phase-space distributions [4].

**References**

[1] E. Descamps, N. Fabre, A. Saharyan, A. Keller, and P. Milman, “Superselection Rules and Bosonic Quantum Computational Resources,” Phys. Rev. Lett. 133, 260605 (2024). <br>
[2] A. Saharyan, E. Descamps, A. Keller, and P. Milman, “Resources for bosonic metrology: Quantum-enhanced precision from a superselection rule perspective,” Phys. Rev. A 113, 022428 (2026). <br>
[3] N. Moulonguet, E. Descamps, J. Lorgeré, A. Saharyan, A. Keller, and P. Milman, “Non-Gaussianity from Superselection Rules,” Phys. Rev. Lett. 137, 050203 (2026). <br>
[4] E. Descamps, A. Saharyan, A. Keller, and P. Milman, “Phase-space representation of classical simulability for arbitrary bosonic encodings,” arXiv:2512.05603 (2025).

</div>

<div id="talk-philippe-codognet" class="talk-entry talk-detail" markdown="1">

#### Comparing different QUBO models for hard combinatorial problems in Quantum Optimization.
{: .talk-title }

Philippe Codognet · JFLI, CNRS, Sorbonne University, The University of Tokyo, NII
{: .talk-byline }

The Costas Arrays Problem, i.e., finding 2-dimensional arrays with optimal auto-correlation properties, is a hard combinatorial problem with many applications in remote sensing and telecommunications. We compare three models for the Costas Array Problem in the formalism of Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO), aimed at an implementation on quantum computers, especially in the quantum annealing approach. The first model is a simple and direct Boolean model in HUBO (Higher-Order Boolean Optimization), then quadratized in QUBO, which requires O(n^4) Boolean variables for a Costas array problem instance of size n. The second and third models are based on an integer-based model and take advantage of using the so-called ”difference triangle” in order to enforce the Costas Array property. For an instance of size n, these new models require O(n^3) Boolean variables and are thus more compact than the direct Boolean model. The difference between those two models is that one uses the one-hot encoding for integer-to-Boolean encoding, while the second uses the domain-wall/unary encoding. Experimental results on both quantum hardware (D-Wave computer) and quantum-inspired annealing hardware (Fixstars Amplify Annealing Engine) show that the domain-wall/unary model performs better than the one-hot model and both perform better than direct Boolean model. This highlights the interest, in quantum optimization, of investigating different QUBO models for the same problem and comparing their performance.

</div>

#### Cross-Validation of Open-Source Quantum Network Simulators
{: .talk-title }

Michal Hajdušek · Keio University
{: .talk-byline }

In this talk, I will perform a comparative analysis of two open-source quantum network simulators, QuISP and SeQUeNCe, focusing on cross-validation of basic networking tasks to ensure consistency and accuracy in simulation outputs. Although both simulators are designed to study quantum network management policies and their dynamics under complex noise models, their differing underlying assumptions can lead to variations in simulation results. <div id="talk-maria-kvashchuk" class="talk-entry talk-detail" markdown="1">

#### The most discriminable quantum states in the multicopy regime
{: .talk-title }

Maria Kvashchuk · LIP6, CNRS, Sorbonne University, NII
{: .talk-byline }

This work investigates which sets of quantum states give rise to the highest achievable success probability in minimum-error state discrimination if multiple copies of the unknown state are given. Specifically, we consider k copies of N uniformly distributed states in dimension d, and derive universal limits in this scenario. For pure state ensembles, we prove that whenever N is large enough to support a state k-design, these designs will exactly give rise to the maximally discriminable sets. We further show that when N exceeds the size required for a k-design, mixed states can outperform all pure state ensembles. We then recognise that the problem of most discriminable classical states in the multi-copy regime is in one-to-one correspondence to the concept of the multiplicative Bayes capacity of independent uses of classical channels, a concept that emerges naturally in the context of classical information leakage. This connection allows us to completely solve the classical analogue of our problem when N is greater then or equals the dimension of the symmetric subspace, and to prove that quantum systems offer a quadratic advantage (in number of copies k) over classical ones. Then, we prove that this quantum over classical advantage is strongly reduced when one is restricted to real quantum states, more precisely, when N≥k+1, pure real qubits only offer a constant advantage over classical bits. Finally, we introduce computational techniques to find sets of most discriminable ensembles and to obtain rigorous universal upper bounds on the maximal success probability for multi-copy state discrimination in cases that are analytically intractable.

</div>

<div id="talk-armando-angrisani" class="talk-entry talk-detail" markdown="1">

#### Polynomial-time classical simulation of boson and fermion sampling under thermal loss
{: .talk-title }

Armando Angrisani · University of Copenhagen → DIENS, CNRS, École normale supérieure
{: .talk-byline }

Sampling experiments are a leading approach to demonstrating quantum computational advantage, yet their robustness to experimental noise is not completely understood. We develop a common framework for classical simulation of boson and fermion sampling under thermal loss, including pure loss. For Fock-state boson sampling, Gaussian boson sampling with fixed uniform squeezing, and fermion sampling with magic inputs, we obtain polynomial-time sampling at inverse-polynomial total variation error, for any fixed nonzero overall loss rate, and with high probability over Haar-random interferometers. A milder flatness assumption on the ideal output distribution suffices for quasipolynomial-time simulation without Haar randomness. With constant thermal loss per layer, the same flatness assumption gives polynomial-time simulation at logarithmic depth. Our bounds for Fock-state boson sampling cover both dilute and saturated regimes and become stronger near full input filling: when only a bounded number of input modes are empty, the noisy output for Haar-random interferometers converges, as the number of modes grows, to a distribution independent of the interferometer. Based on [arXiv:2611.08444](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08444).

</div>

<div id="talk-ravi-kunjwal" class="talk-entry talk-detail" markdown="1">

#### Quantum circuits via classical functions
{: .talk-title }

Ravi Kunjwal · LIS, CNRS, Aix-Marseille University
{: .talk-byline }

It is well-known that every n-qubit unitary can be decomposed into a product of controlled single-qubit unitaries. We show that the control conditions in such a decomposition naturally define a classical function that we term a quasi-process function, motivated by its role in the study of indefinite causal order. Here quasi-process functions yield a representation of every n-qubit unitary in a canonical form, namely, as a quasi-process function unitary (qPFU). We use this representation to show how quantum nonlocality without entanglement (QNLWE) can be understood in terms of unitaries that encode the trade-off between causal order and locality noted in R. Kunjwal and Ä. Baumeler, [arXiv:2202.00440](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.00440). Specifically, we obtain two sufficient (but not necessary) criteria---mutual exclusivity of control conditions (MECC) and Unambiguity---for a unitary to be a process function unitary (PFU), i.e., it admits a qPFU representation where the quasi-process function is a process function, an object that can model paradox-free classical causal loops. We also show the inequivalence of these criteria, neither implying the other. We then show that the circuit depth of MECC unitaries admits a characterization in terms of clique-partitioning problems on Hamming graphs and use this correspondence to provide bounds on the circuit depth of n-qubit MECC unitaries. Finally, we show how MECC unitaries provide a recipe for constructing a unitary purification of the associated process function, thereby providing a large class of unitary processes of interest in quantum causality. The generality of this causality-inspired representation of unitaries also makes it a versatile tool for analyzing the structure of quantum circuits in other domains. This talk is based on joint work with Nasra D. Ahmed, [arXiv:2610.00579 (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00579).

</div>

<div id="talk-romain-piron" class="talk-entry talk-detail" markdown="1">

#### Calibration Method For Reduced-Bandwidth Light-Matter Simulations
{: .talk-title }

Romain Piron · NII
{: .talk-byline }

Photons are natural carriers of quantum information, making photonic technologies essential building blocks for quantum communication and distributed quantum computing. Understanding how photons propagate and interact with matter at the quantum level is therefore important for modeling and designing quantum-network components. Hamiltonian-based microscopic models provide a natural framework for describing these interactions, but their analytical treatment is often challenging, making numerical simulations a valuable tool. However, such simulations can become computationally demanding due to the large number of photonic modes involved. In this talk, I will present our recent work on improving the computational efficiency of light–matter simulations while preserving their physical accuracy. We show that restricting the spectral range of photonic modes requires a consistent calibration of model parameters to avoid altering simulation results. We demonstrate this calibration strategy through single- and two-photon scattering simulations across different geometries, taking steps towards efficient microscopic simulations of light-matter systems, with the long-term goal of modeling and emulating optical components for quantum networks.

</div>

<div id="talk-ivan-supic" class="talk-entry talk-detail" markdown="1">

#### Computational Informativeness and Incompatibility of Quantum Measurements
{: .talk-title }

Ivan Šupić · LIG, CNRS, University Grenoble Alpes
{: .talk-byline }

The informativeness of a quantum measurement and the incompatibility of a family of measurements are quantified by robustness measures, which equal the largest advantage the measurements give in state-discrimination tasks. These measures are information-theoretic, since any state may reveal the resource and any joint measurement may simulate a compatible family. We introduce computational versions in which the states must be efficiently preparable and a joint measurement must consist of a parent measurement and classical post-processing, both of polynomial cost, and show that the identities with discrimination survive these restrictions. Assuming QMA ≠ QCMA, that is, that quantum proofs are more powerful than classical ones, the computational and information-theoretic notions separate in both directions. One family of efficiently implementable measurements is nearly maximally informative and incompatible, yet every experiment with polynomial-cost preparations, adaptive ones included, sees only a negligible advantage. Conversely, there are exactly compatible measurements, each efficiently implementable, that act as incompatible for every efficient user, because every efficient joint implementation errs by an inverse-polynomial amount on efficiently preparable separable states.

</div>

<div id="talk-mehdi-mhalla" class="talk-entry talk-detail" markdown="1">

#### Interrupting the Link between Correlations and Probabilities: Contextuality from Open Dynamical Processes
{: .talk-title }

Mehdi Mhalla · LIG, CNRS, University Grenoble Alpes
{: .talk-byline }

Recent advances in the study of contextuality have provided increasingly refined quantitative characterizations of non-classical correlations. In particular, our work on the multipartiteness width of contextuality naturally raises a more fundamental question: what should count as a "part" in a physical or informational process? More generally, within an explanatory framework, what are the minimal representational structures that the theory does not exclude a priori and that may therefore be considered, at the meta-language level, as observers, agents, or simply parts? Motivated by this question, we initiated, through Louis Triouleyre's Master's project, a study of contextuality from a dynamical perspective. Instead of taking measurement scenarios as primitive, we study contextual behaviours generated by interacting open automata, whose observable behaviours emerge through their interactions. This ongoing work aims at providing a process-oriented interpretation of contextuality. We show how the contextual fraction can be recovered when lifting the description from observed states to execution paths generated by open automata. Finally, I will briefly discuss the broader research program motivating this direction. Rather than taking observers, subsystems and representations as primitive notions, we aim to investigate how they may all emerge collectively as localized perspectives within interruptible interaction processes. The long-term objective is to develop a common framework in which contextuality, distributed computation, information processing and language are understood as different aspects of the dynamics of representation.

</div>

<div id="talk-zoe-g-del-toro" class="talk-entry talk-detail" markdown="1">

#### Purification as an Operational Resource
{: .talk-title }

Zoe G. del Toro · LIP6, CNRS, Sorbonne University
{: .talk-byline }

Purifications provide a powerful way of representing quantum states and channels, but which purifications can actually be obtained when the underlying resource is only available through black-box uses?

We will first discuss the possibility of constructing purifications of unknown quantum states and channels, showing that no finite number of uses can produce a fixed unknown purification exactly. One may then ask whether this obstruction can be overcome by averaging over the unknown purifying degrees of freedom. While such an averaged notion captures the relevant information in parallel settings, we show that it also fails in the sequential setting. This is witnessed operationally through channel discrimination: we exhibit perfect discrimination using two sequential queries to the purified channels and a query to the bare channel that cannot be achieved by any finite number of queries only to the bare channel, as well as strict gaps in two-query discrimination. These results reveal a fundamental distinction between bare and purified access to quantum channels.

</div>

<div id="talk-kristin-s-lien" class="talk-entry talk-detail" markdown="1">

#### Entanglement in quantum channel discrimination: sometimes less is more
{: .talk-title }

Kristin S. Lien · LIP6, CNRS, Sorbonne University
{: .talk-byline }

Entanglement is known to be a powerful resource that improves performance in various quantum information and computational tasks. A standard example that demonstrates the usefulness of entanglement is the possibility of perfectly discriminating between all four Pauli operations in a single shot via the superdense coding protocol. While entanglement often improves quantum channel discrimination, this is not necessarily the case. In this work, we identify scenarios in which a maximally entangled state is a bad choice of input state and, more generally, we show that excessive entanglement can reduce channel discriminability dramatically. For instance, we present an explicit pair of unitary channels that are perfectly discriminable without entanglement, but for which any strategy with a maximally entangled input state is ϵ-close to a blind uniform guessing strategy. We introduce the concepts of Maximal Entanglement Best Case (MEBC) and Maximal Entanglement Worst Case (MEWC) pairs of channels, characterising channel discrimination tasks where a maximally entangled input state is optimal or as suboptimal as it can be, respectively. For both of these cases, we present conditions and explicit examples. Furthermore, we show that the optimal input states for discriminating MEWC pairs of channels are necessarily separable, and provide non-trivial examples of measurement channels for which any entanglement necessarily reduces the maximum probability of discrimination.

</div>

<div id="talk-ryuji-takagi" class="talk-entry talk-detail" markdown="1">

#### Universal distillation of quantum entanglement
{: .talk-title }

Ryuji Takagi · The University of Tokyo
{: .talk-byline }

Distillable entanglement has been a central quantity to characterize the operational value of entangled states. However, this operational meaning had been known to be valid only under a crucial assumption: both parties know the description of the input state and can choose the protocol tailored to it, which does not necessarily reflect the natural operational setting. In this talk, I present a universal LOCC protocol---whose description does not depend on the input state and thus works universally for any input---that achieves the distillable entanglement of an arbitrary unknown input state. Our result therefore lifts the distillable entanglement to a robust notion of an operationally meaningful quantity and clarifies that input-state knowledge is not necessary to achieve the optimal rate of entanglement distillation.

</div>

<div id="talk-satoshi-yoshida" class="talk-entry talk-detail" markdown="1">

#### Random dilation superchannel: Go and no-go results
{: .talk-title }

Satoshi Yoshida · The University of Tokyo
{: .talk-byline }

We present a quantum circuit that implements the random dilation superchannel, transforming parallel queries of an unknown quantum channel into the same number of parallel queries of a randomly chosen dilation isometry of the input channel. This is a natural generalization of the random purification channel, which transforms copies of an unknown mixed state into copies of a randomly chosen purification state. The circuit complexity of our construction is O(poly(n, log d\_I, log d\_O)), where n is the number of queries, and d\_I and d\_O are the input and output dimensions of the input channel, respectively. This random dilation superchannel is extended to the sequential queries approximately, by transforming the parallel random dilation isometry into sequential random dilation unitaries with O(poly(d\_I)) overhead in the number of queries. We also show that our results can be further extended to random dilation of quantum combs. On the other hand, we show a no-go theorem on the exact random dilation of sequential queries with o(poly(min\{d\_I,d\_O\})) query overhead, showcasing a fundamental difference between the parallel and sequential cases.
